Researchers identify MiniMax in AI prompt data-access investigation

A Reuters investigation published on 26 November identified MiniMax as the AI provider behind a researcher’s earlier findings that private links in prompts were accessed, including links in requests marked for zero data retention. MiniMax says it disabled the automated URL-checking process.

Raazim Tech Editorial DeskPublished 27 November 2026 · 08:33 EAT6 min read

A cybersecurity investigation into how AI services handle private prompts has identified Chinese AI developer MiniMax as the provider whose systems accessed test links embedded in user-style requests. The new identification was reported by Reuters on 26 November, adding the company’s name and further details to research that Dragos had published without naming the provider a week earlier.

The finding matters because some of the test requests carried Zero Data Retention (ZDR) or no-data-collection settings. The researcher says MiniMax systems still fetched the unique URLs placed in those prompts. That does not establish that every customer prompt was read, or that complete conversations were disclosed. It does show that privacy promises can become complicated when an AI request passes through several providers.

What the researchers observed

Dragos chief research officer HD Moore told Reuters that the company sent nearly two million requests containing unique, trackable links and test credentials to AI providers between July and November 2026. By monitoring those links, the researchers could see when systems outside their own environment attempted to access information that had appeared only in a test prompt.

For the provider later identified as MiniMax, Dragos said links were fetched from more than half of the prompts it sent to the company’s models between July and October. Roughly two-thirds of the requests in that group were marked as ZDR or no data collection. The access usually appeared two or three days after the original request and occurred when traffic reached MiniMax’s cloud, including traffic routed through other services.

Reuters also reported two additional observations from Moore. A secret link appeared in a Feishu workplace chat channel 24 days after it had been submitted and was then opened by devices in Beijing and Shanghai. Researchers also saw attempts to download private software-code collections referenced in MiniMax requests. They could not establish who initiated those attempts or whether any code was obtained.

MiniMax says an automated security process opened some links

In a statement dated 24 November, MiniMax said its internal security monitoring extracted URLs and IP addresses from certain requests forwarded by inference providers. Automated security programs then checked some of the recorded URLs for potential threats. The company said the mechanism was enabled by default on the relevant MiniMax endpoints, including for requests carrying ZDR or no-data-collection flags.

MiniMax says the system did not retain full HTTP request or response bodies. It also says it found no evidence, in the requests it examined, that full prompt content or model responses were disclosed to third parties beyond the URL activity it described. The company said it disabled the automated process, stopped the activity on the identified path for current business-to-business traffic and deleted the URLs stored in its security logs.

The company’s explanation does not settle every point raised by the researcher. Reuters reported that MiniMax’s 24 November statement did not address the Feishu chat observation or the later attempts to retrieve private code repositories. Those events remain unexplained in the public response reviewed for this report.

AI routing providers are changing their handling

Atlas Cloud said its own review was consistent with the links being accessed after requests reached MiniMax, rather than on Atlas Cloud’s infrastructure. The service stopped forwarding requests to MiniMax’s official API and advised customers who had used MiniMax M3, M2.7 or M3.1 Flash through the affected route to treat secrets included in those prompts as potentially exposed. It recommended reviewing prompts and outputs and rotating credentials where necessary.

OpenRouter separately said it removed three inference providers from its platform, removed 125 endpoints from Zero Data Retention eligibility and notified potentially affected customers. It said the research did not show a breach of OpenRouter’s own systems. The 125-endpoint action included precautionary removals and should not be read as 125 confirmed MiniMax exposures.

Why this matters to developers and businesses

AI applications are often sold through model gateways, marketplaces and resellers. A developer may select a model through one dashboard while the request is forwarded to another company’s API or hosting environment. Each hop can have its own logging, security checks, retention settings and subprocessors.

A ZDR label at one layer is not a guarantee that every downstream system handles every field in the same way. Even when a provider says it does not keep complete conversations, URLs and other metadata can reveal private resources. A signed download link, an internal dashboard address or a repository URL may grant access without exposing a password in plain text.

This is relevant to small development teams as well as large organizations. Developers building chatbots, coding assistants or AI features into business systems should understand where requests are processed, which providers can inspect them and how privacy settings apply after routing.

Practical steps for AI users

  1. Keep secrets out of prompts. Do not paste production API keys, passwords, session tokens or private signed URLs into a hosted model request.
  2. Review any affected traffic. If you used MiniMax M3, M2.7 or M3.1 Flash through Atlas Cloud’s affected official-API route, follow Atlas Cloud’s notice and inspect prompts and outputs for credentials or private links.
  3. Rotate exposed credentials. Revoke and replace any key, token or password that may have been included in a request. Do not assume deleting chat history revokes a credential.
  4. Check the full routing chain. Ask whether an aggregator forwards requests to the model developer’s own API, which subprocessors are involved and whether the same privacy controls apply at each hop.
  5. Use least privilege and redaction. Give AI tools only the access they need, remove sensitive fields before sending data and keep production secrets in a dedicated secrets manager.

Teams that depend on confidential code or customer records should also review their contracts and provider documentation rather than relying on a model name or a single privacy badge in a dashboard.

Raazim Tech takeaway

The important lesson is not that every AI provider is unsafe. It is that prompt privacy depends on the entire inference path. Know which systems receive the request, avoid sending credentials and private links, and treat a zero-retention label as a policy claim that needs to be verified across every provider involved.

What remains unknown

The public evidence does not establish how many real customer accounts were affected, whether any private customer repository was successfully downloaded or who was responsible for the Feishu post. MiniMax says it found no evidence of full prompt or response disclosure in the requests it examined, while the researcher’s separate observations have not been fully explained by the company. Those distinctions matter: URL access is confirmed by the researcher’s tests and acknowledged as automated activity by MiniMax, but broader theft of customer conversations has not been established.
