Hackers breach US Air Force Cloud One platform, report says

A Reuters investigation published on 27 November says hackers breached the Air Force’s multi-cloud platform. The intrusion’s scope, the attacker and any data theft remain unknown.

Raazim Tech Editorial DeskPublished 28 November 2026 · 08:36 EAT5 min read

Hackers breached Cloud One, the U.S. Air Force’s enterprise cloud platform, according to a Reuters investigation published on 27 November 2026. The intrusion was discovered during work to upgrade the system and has disrupted some operations, but the available reporting does not establish who was responsible, how much access the attackers obtained or whether they stole data.

The report is significant because Cloud One is not a single application or a standalone data centre. It is a shared, multi-cloud environment that supports a range of Air Force systems. A security incident affecting its management layer could therefore have consequences beyond one service, although the full impact in this case remains unconfirmed.

What is known about the breach

Three people familiar with the matter told Reuters that the intrusion was found during an upgrade within the month before the report was published. Federal News Network separately reported on 27 November that the Air Force and Cloud One contractors discovered the breach while patching a vulnerability in the cloud-hosting environment. Neither report gave an exact date for the intrusion or its discovery.

According to Reuters, investigators brought in U.S. Cyber Command to assess the damage. The discovery also led to operational disruption: sources said migration of back-office applications was paused, updates to applications already running on Cloud One were blocked, and some users lost administrator rights while the incident was being addressed.

One source told Reuters that the attackers used a software vulnerability to reach the platform’s control plane. In cloud environments, the control plane is the management layer used to configure and administer resources. Access to it can be sensitive because it may provide visibility or control across multiple workloads. However, Reuters said it could not identify the vulnerability involved, and the reporting does not show that attackers used this access to inspect aircraft status, logistics movements or other operational data.

Why Cloud One matters

The Air Force says Cloud One is a multi-cloud environment for Department of Defense mission application owners. Its official platform description lists commercial cloud services and shared capabilities for application hosting, migration, security and data management.

Reuters reported that the platform, initiated in 2017, combines services from Amazon, Microsoft, Oracle and Google. Applications supported by Cloud One include systems handling personnel information and pay, logistics and maintenance, air-defence radar data, and munitions tracking across more than 200 Air Force locations.

Leidos announced in March 2026 that it had a $454.9 million contract to modernise Cloud One with Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud Platform and Oracle Cloud Infrastructure. The company said the work was intended to improve security, automation and day-to-day operations. Leidos referred questions about the reported breach to the Air Force, according to Reuters.

The Air Force has not confirmed the scope publicly

The Air Force declined to discuss the security posture, vulnerabilities or operational status of specific networks when Reuters asked about the incident. Its statement said it takes measures to protect department information, systems and mission capabilities. That is not a confirmation of the suspected access path, and it does not answer whether sensitive records were taken.

The public accounts also leave several important questions open. There is no named attacker, no disclosed CVE, no confirmed list of affected applications and no public accounting of data exfiltration. The use of a vulnerability to reach the control plane is attributed to one source, not to a published technical incident report. Those details should not be treated as established facts beyond what the reporting attributes to its sources.

What cloud operators can learn

The incident is a reminder that a cloud security boundary includes more than the virtual machines and applications running inside it. The administrative plane, identity system, automation tools and update process can be equally important. A compromise of a management layer may increase the potential blast radius, particularly where multiple environments share privileged identities or deployment workflows.

  • Separate management access. Keep administrative interfaces off the public internet where possible, and restrict access through hardened, monitored paths.
  • Use least privilege. Give operators and automation identities only the permissions needed for their tasks. Review privileged accounts and remove unused access.
  • Protect the control plane. Enforce strong multi-factor authentication, short-lived credentials, approval for sensitive changes and detailed audit logging.
  • Keep incident response independent. Maintain backups, recovery procedures and emergency access that do not depend entirely on the management systems being investigated.
  • Patch without losing visibility. Stage updates carefully, but preserve monitoring and forensic logs so a remediation effort does not erase evidence of earlier access.

These are general defensive practices, not claims about which controls Cloud One did or did not have. The public reporting has not disclosed the technical details needed to assess the platform’s configuration.

Raazim Tech takeaway

For organisations using cloud services, the lesson is to secure the management layer as carefully as the applications it controls. Multi-cloud architecture can improve flexibility, but it does not automatically isolate risk when identities, automation or administration are shared.

What happens next

The next meaningful update should clarify the intrusion path, the systems affected and whether any data was accessed or removed. Until the Air Force or investigators publish more detail, the breach should be treated as a serious reported incident with an unresolved scope, not as proof that every Cloud One workload or every cloud provider involved was compromised.
